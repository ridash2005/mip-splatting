\section{Gate G2 --- the defect it found}
\label{sec:g2}

G2 was declared in advance as: 3DGS falls $\gtrsim13$\,dB from full to
$\tfrac18$; Mip-Splatting falls $\lesssim6$\,dB; the gap at $\tfrac18$ exceeds
$9$\,dB. On R1 it failed, on two of its three criteria: arm B fell $9.09$\,dB
where $13$ was required, and the gap at $\tfrac18$ was $4.07$\,dB against a
published $10.98$. The protocol's instruction on a G2 failure is to stop and
diagnose in a stated order, and that is what follows. It took two attempts,
and the first was wrong.

\subsection*{The three checks the protocol names, and a fourth it does not}

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.4em,itemsep=2pt]
  \item \textbf{The splitter's index mapping.} Its count-weighted mean reproduces
        \texttt{metrics.py}'s pooled PSNR to within $0.01$\,dB on every scene of
        every run --- asserted per scene, not spot-checked. Not the cause.
  \item \textbf{Arm B's \texttt{filter\_3D} at step 30\,000.} Read back from all
        eight saved point clouds it is identically zero, and non-zero on all
        eight of arm A's. The reduction is exact. Not the cause.
  \item \textbf{\texttt{--white\_background}.} Passed to every job. Not the cause.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{The first diagnosis, and why it was wrong.} With those three
eliminated, the explanation offered was densification: this repository's
\texttt{densify\_and\_prune} splits on a quantile over accumulated
\emph{absolute} gradients, which is the Gaussian Opacity Fields criterion added
to \texttt{mip-splatting} in commit \texttt{24af724} (April 2024), after the
Mip-Splatting paper's numbers were produced. Original 3DGS splits on
$\lVert\nabla\rVert \ge \tau_{\mathrm{pos}}$ alone. The argument was that a
better densification recovers most of what the missing band-limit costs, so the
collapse G2's thresholds were calibrated against could not occur in this
codebase --- and that G2 was therefore unmeetable here as a property of the
gate's premise rather than of the experiment.

It was a plausible story, it was consistent with every number then available,
and it was \emph{wrong}. It also had the shape a wrong diagnosis usually has:
it explained the failure as unfixable, and it did so without a test that could
have refuted it.

\subsection*{The fourth check: arm B was not running 3DGS's rasteriser}

The single-codebase construction of \S\ref{sec:onecodebase} gives both arms
Mip-Splatting's rasteriser, and sets arm B apart by zeroing \texttt{filter\_3D}
and taking \texttt{kernel\_size}~$=0.3$. That reproduces 3DGS's \emph{3D}
band-limit exactly --- which is what check~2 verifies --- and says nothing about
the \emph{2D} one. Mip-Splatting's screen-space filter dilates the projected
covariance by $k$ and then compensates the opacity by
\begin{equation}
  \rho \;=\; \sqrt{\frac{\lvert\Sigma'\rvert}{\lvert\Sigma' + kI\rvert}} ,
\end{equation}
so that dilation does not brighten the primitive. Vanilla 3DGS applies the same
fixed $0.3$ dilation and \textbf{no compensation at all}. Setting
\texttt{kernel\_size}~$=0.3$ reproduces the dilation magnitude and leaves $\rho$
in place, so arm B kept an anti-aliasing term that 3DGS does not have --- and
the term it kept is precisely the one that protects a dilated primitive from
losing energy as the test sampling rate falls. An arm that anti-aliases better
than 3DGS degrades less than 3DGS, which is exactly what G2 measured.

\paragraph{C1 --- the test that separates the two explanations.} $\rho$ is
computed in CUDA, so the switch is a rasteriser change: a
\texttt{mip\_compensation} flag threaded through
\texttt{GaussianRasterizationSettings} into \texttt{computeCov2D}, which skips
the $\rho$ term while keeping the dilation. Everything else in that function is
identical to \texttt{graphdeco-inria/diff-gaussian-rasterization} line for line.
The build asserts the compiled binary carries the field before any training
starts, because checking the source would prove nothing about the binary.

One scene, \texttt{lego}, 30\,000 iterations, everything else held fixed:

\input{generated/table-c1}

Full resolution barely moves: $\cOneBaseFull$ against $\cOneFull$, which is
what must happen, because at the training sampling rate the dilation is
negligible against the primitive's own extent and $\rho \approx 1$. Every
reduced scale collapses, and lands on the shape of the published 3DGS row:
$\cOneHalf$ against $26.95$, $\cOneQuarter$ against $21.38$, $\cOneEighth$
against $17.69$. The compensation was the whole of the defect.

\keybox{the densification hypothesis is refuted, not merely superseded}{%
Both columns of Table~\ref{tab:c1} ran GOF's densification. If densification were
the cause of the shortfall, the right-hand column could not have collapsed --- and
it collapses by $\cOneCollapse$\,dB at $\tfrac18$, landing within $0.3$\,dB of
the published figure at every reduced scale. The earlier diagnosis is therefore
wrong, and it is left in this chapter rather than deleted because how a wrong
diagnosis survived matters: it was never given an experiment that could refute
it. It explained the anomaly as a property of the codebase, which is the most
comfortable kind of explanation and the one that most deserves a test.

Worth stating separately, because it is easy to read the wrong way: the
comparison that settles this is the one \emph{between the two columns}, not
between either column and the published row. Both columns are \texttt{lego} and
the published row is an eight-scene mean, so the agreement at reduced scales is
corroboration and not a measurement. The measurement is that switching one CUDA
term moved $\tfrac18$ by $\cOneCollapse$\,dB and full resolution by less than
$0.05$.}

\subsection*{G2, re-scored}

Every arm-B row produced with the compensation active is superseded. They are
marked \texttt{SUPERSEDED\_BY\_C2} in \texttt{results/runs.csv} and retained ---
every table in this document excludes them, and none of them has been deleted.
Arm A is untouched and was not re-run: nothing about this defect involves it.

\input{generated/table-g2}

All three criteria pass, against thresholds that were set before any run and have
not been touched since. And the consequence reaches further than the gate: with
the arm rebuilt, Table~\ref{tab:stmt} reproduces \emph{both} baselines rather
than one. 3DGS lands within $0.42$\,dB of its published figure at every test
scale and Mip-Splatting within $0.50$, and the gap column --- the quantity the
whole two-arm construction exists to measure --- reproduces to within $0.2$\,dB
at all four. This project did not have that before; it had arm A at L1 and an arm
B that was several decibels adrift for a reason it had explained incorrectly.

\keybox{what this cost, and what it bought}{%
It cost the R1 arm-B column, the R2 arm-B column and the arm-B half of the seed
spread --- three rungs of measurement, re-run, and it surfaced a numerical
fragility that had been hidden by the compensation (\S\ref{sec:shipfails}). It
bought a 3DGS arm that is actually 3DGS in both band-limits rather than one, so
the control every later comparison rests on is the method and not a residual
anti-aliasing term. Worth restating plainly: the between-arm comparison was the
one thing the earlier diagnosis insisted was safe, and it was the thing that was
wrong.}

\subsection*{What the vanilla arm cost in robustness}
\label{sec:shipfails}

Removing the compensation also removed a guard. The same CUDA branch that
computes $\rho$ carries \texttt{coef = 0} for a primitive whose projected
covariance is singular to the tolerance the determinants are clamped at --- a
numerical guard, not part of the anti-aliasing model. With the compensation
switched off, degenerate primitives survived at full opacity with an unbounded
screen-space radius, and two scenes died reproducibly, on both GPUs, with a CUDA
illegal memory access around iteration $3\,000$. Six trained normally, which is
what made it look transient.

Hoisting the guard out of the branch fixed one of the two. The other then failed
in the \emph{backward} pass instead, because the backward kernel still had its
copy of the guard inside the branch: the forward was contributing nothing for a
degenerate primitive while a gradient still flowed to its opacity. Zeroing a
forward contribution and leaving its gradient live is not a consistent pair, and
matching them is what the C1 switch should have done from the start --- it was
supposed to isolate one term and it removed three.

\keybox{a switch that is not minimal is not a control}{%
Each of those three removals was invisible: no assertion covered them, the
numbers that came back were plausible, and six of eight scenes were unaffected.
What exposed them was a failure that could not be waved through --- a crash --- and
then a failure that \emph{moved} from the forward pass to the backward one when
half the fix was applied, which is what identified the second half. A switch
meant to isolate a single term has to be checked against what it leaves alone,
not only against what it changes.}

\section{What remains of the densification difference}
\label{sec:densification}

Section~\ref{sec:g2} settles the cause of the $\tfrac18$-scale shortfall: it was
the 2D opacity compensation, and removing it recovers the published shape on the
scene it was tested on. That does not make the densification observation false,
only insufficient, and it is worth keeping straight what it does and does not
imply.

\keybox{the repository's densification is still not 3DGS's}{%
\texttt{densify\_and\_prune} in this codebase splits on a quantile over
accumulated \emph{absolute} gradients:
\texttt{grads\_abs} is taken from \texttt{viewspace\_point\_tensor.grad[:,\,2:]}
and thresholded at $Q = \mathrm{quantile}(\texttt{grads\_abs},\,1-\varrho)$,
where $\varrho$ is the fraction of primitives passing the ordinary gradient
test. That is the Gaussian Opacity Fields criterion, added to
\texttt{mip-splatting} in commit \texttt{24af724} (April 2024) --- \emph{after}
the Mip-Splatting paper's numbers were produced. Original 3DGS splits on
$\lVert\nabla\rVert \geq \tau_{\mathrm{pos}}$ alone. This is a real difference
from published 3DGS and it is shared by both arms.}

What it explains is the level at full resolution, not the slope across scales.
Arm B reaches $\cOneBaseFull$\,dB on \texttt{lego} at full scale both with and
without the compensation (Table~\ref{tab:c1}), against a published eight-scene
mean of $33.33$; a better densification plausibly accounts for standing above the
published level, and nothing about it changes when a screen-space opacity term is
switched off. What it cannot explain is the $\tfrac18$-scale behaviour, because
both columns of Table~\ref{tab:c1} carry the identical densification and differ
by $\cOneCollapse$\,dB there. Level and slope are separate quantities, and the
earlier reading of this section conflated them.

Three consequences, revised:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.4em,itemsep=3pt]
  \item \textbf{Arm B still carries no L1 claim against published 3DGS.} The
        densification differs, so the published column in Tables~\ref{tab:stmt}
        and~\ref{tab:mtmt} remains a reference point of a different
        configuration for arm B and a target only for arm A. Its deltas are
        reported and are not reproduction error.
  \item \textbf{The between-arm comparison is now what it was claimed to be.}
        Before C1 it was not: the arms differed by the 2D compensation as well as
        by the 3D filter, so the gap column mixed the method with a residual
        anti-aliasing term. Both arms now share the densification, the
        dataloader, the metrics, the LPIPS backbone \emph{and} the screen-space
        filter semantics appropriate to each, and the difference between them is
        the band-limit.
  \item \textbf{The published $\tfrac18$ gap is reproducible after all.}
        Section~\ref{sec:g2} scores it. The earlier claim that it could not be
        reproduced in this codebase was a consequence of the defect, not of the
        codebase, and it is the clearest illustration in this project of why an
        explanation that makes a failure unfixable should be distrusted until it
        has been given a chance to fail.
\end{enumerate}

Pinning the pre-GOF commit and re-running both arms would remove the remaining
difference from published 3DGS, at the cost of a densification that no longer
matches the Mip-Splatting arm --- which would move the difference between the
arms out of the filter and back into the plumbing. Given that
Table~\ref{tab:levels} makes the self-run baseline the control, the
shared-densification choice is still the right one, and the residual is stated
rather than removed.

\section{Run-to-run spread (I-3)}
\label{sec:spread}
Parity is not a testable claim without a $\sigma$. Repeats of an identical
configuration bound the harness's own nondeterminism --- CUDA atomics in the
rasteriser accumulate in a non-deterministic order, so bit-identical inputs do
not give bit-identical outputs.

\input{generated/table-seedspread}

Two different quantities are at stake here, and separating them turns out to
matter.

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.4em,itemsep=3pt]
  \item \textbf{Nondeterminism at a fixed seed} --- the same configuration run
        twice. CUDA atomics in the rasteriser accumulate in a non-deterministic
        order, so bit-identical inputs do not give bit-identical outputs. The
        largest per-scale difference observed is $\repeatSpread$\,dB.
  \item \textbf{Seed-to-seed variation} --- a different initial point cloud and a
        different view order. Three seeds on two scenes, both arms, all four
        scales, give a largest sample standard deviation of
        $\mathbf{\seedSigma}$\,dB, at \seedSigmaWhere.
\end{enumerate}

The second is roughly three and a half times the first, so the initialisation
draw matters considerably more than the atomics --- which is not obvious in
advance, and is the reason R5 is marked not optional in the protocol.

\keybox{the seed spread is not comfortably below the L1 tolerance}{%
L1 allows $\pm0.20$\,dB. A worst-case seed standard deviation of
$\seedSigma$\,dB is not comfortably below that, so a single-seed L1 claim at an
unfavourable scale is marginal on its own terms. A three-seed mean has a
standard error of $\seedSigma/\sqrt{3}$ and is comfortable; a single run is not. Arm A's L1
reproduction in \S\ref{sec:table1} is a single-seed result, and its margins
($0.01$ to $0.09$\,dB) are of the same order as this $\sigma$. It should be read
as L1 attained, not as L1 attained with room to spare.}

The spread is also not uniform: the worst case over all four scales is
$\seedSigmaArmB$\,dB for 3DGS and $\seedSigmaArmA$\,dB for Mip-Splatting. The
arm without a band-limit is the less stable one under reseeding, which is
consistent with the mechanism this thesis proposes, though two scenes are far
too few to claim it as a finding.

\subsection*{I-1 on the standard benchmark: the floor binds everywhere}
On the full orbit, the estimation term $s^2/\lambda_i$ exceeds
Mip-Splatting's floor $f_k^2$ on \textbf{zero} primitives, in \emph{every}
direction of \emph{every} scene. The median ratio
$\sqrt{s^2/\lambda_{\min}}\,/\,f_k$ is $0.35$, and even at the 99th percentile
it is below $0.5$: the floor dominates the estimation term by a factor of two to
three throughout. No primitive is unseen by every camera, so the artefactual
right tail of \S\ref{sec:instruments} does not arise and nothing had to be
excluded.

By Proposition~\ref{prop:reduction} this means B1 \emph{is} Mip-Splatting on
these scenes --- exactly, not approximately. That is worth stating plainly:

\keybox{no gain is available on the standard Blender benchmark}{%
The eight Blender scenes are 100-camera full orbits, the best-conditioned
capture in the suite. Section~\ref{sec:anisotropy} predicts parity there, and
the measurement is stronger than the prediction: the anisotropy is $1.14\times$
where Table~\ref{tab:regimes} anticipated $1.6\times$. A method evaluated only
on this benchmark could not show an improvement, and if one appeared it would be
a bug.}

\subsection*{A defect that made two chapters disagree}
\label{sec:branchdefect}

Before the numbers: the instrument results below and the PSNR results of
\S\ref{sec:table3} were, for a time, measurements of \emph{different captures}
reported under the same protocol names, and it is worth saying exactly how,
because nothing in either run could have detected it.

The instrument kernel cloned the repository's default branch. The training
kernel clones the method branch. Between them, the definition of \texttt{arc}
and \texttt{cone} had changed: the default branch sizes them by angular
\emph{width}, giving $20$ cameras across $81^\circ$ and $3$ across $30^\circ$;
the method branch sizes them by camera \emph{count}, giving $25$ across
$93^\circ$ and $10$ across $54^\circ$. The change was deliberate and its stated
reason is sound --- a $20^\circ$ wedge catches three cameras on a Blender
capture, and three views is a sparse-reconstruction experiment rather than a
conditioning one. What was missed is that only one of the two kernels was
updated.

Each run was internally consistent, each printed the span it had built, and each
was correct about its own capture. The error was in reading them side by side,
and it survived because both numbers looked reasonable: a cone \emph{is}
narrower than an arc in both definitions, so the ordering the chapter argues for
held in either. It is the same class of mistake as the arm-B compensation of
\S\ref{sec:g2} --- a quantity that was never what its label said --- and it was
found the same way, by a measurement that could not be reconciled with another
rather than by an assertion firing.

\keybox{what it cost, and the one line that prevents it}{%
It cost the first reading of gates G3 and G4. On the width-based cone the
estimation term exceeds the Nyquist floor on $100\,\%$ of primitives in
\emph{every} direction; on the count-based cone it exceeds it nowhere. Both are
measured, both are right, and only the first is the regime this method is about.
The instrument kernel is now pinned to the same branch the training kernel
clones, and every protocol table in this chapter carries the span the session
that produced the row actually built --- where the instruments and the training
run disagree about a protocol's geometry by more than $2^\circ$, the
conditioning cell is left empty rather than filled from the other capture.}

\subsection*{I-2 across the stress suite: the regime structure is real}
The refutation condition at gate G3 is that the floor exceeds the estimation
term on nearly every primitive in \emph{every} scene --- which would make
Proposition~\ref{prop:reduction} apply universally and collapse the method to
its baseline everywhere. It does not. Degrading the capture moves the balance
exactly as Equation~\eqref{eq:cap} requires:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em,itemsep=2pt]
  \item \textbf{One-sided arc} ($80^\circ$ span): the estimation term exceeds
        the floor along the worst-constrained direction on $100\,\%$ of
        primitives, while still lying below it in the best-constrained
        directions. That is precisely the anisotropic regime the filter is built
        for --- a scalar floor is wrong here in one direction and right in the
        others.
  \item \textbf{Low-parallax cone} ($20^\circ$ span): the estimation term
        exceeds the floor in \emph{all} directions on $100\,\%$ of primitives,
        by a median factor of $12$. The measured
        $\sigma_{\mathrm{depth}}/\sigma_{\mathrm{lat}}$ is $\instConeAniso\times$
        against the $\instConeCap\times$ that Equation~\eqref{eq:cap} predicts at
        that span.
  \item \textbf{Grazing} views degrade the conditioning mildly
        ($\instGrazingAniso\times$ measured, $\instGrazingCap\times$ predicted),
        and \textbf{mixed focal} barely at all ($\instMixedAniso\times$ against
        the full orbit's $\instFullAniso\times$).
\end{itemize}

\keybox{the prediction has to be the cap form, and then it holds}{%
Every measured anisotropy above exceeds the two-view prediction of
Equation~\eqref{eq:anisotropy}, and by roughly the same factor throughout ---
$1.14$ against $1.00$, $2.28$ against $1.56$, $9.45$ against $5.76$. That is not
five independent discrepancies; it is one, and \S\ref{sec:capprediction} names
it. A protocol is a distribution of cameras over an angular window, not two
cameras at its ends, and for a cap the correct $\lambda_3/\lambda_1$ is
asymptotically \emph{half} the two-view value --- so the two-view form
understates $\sigma_D/\sigma_L$ by $\sqrt{2}$. Against Equation~\eqref{eq:cap},
evaluated at each protocol's own measured span, the five rows of
Table~\ref{tab:instruments} agree to $10$--$15\,\%$ instead of being low by
$60\,\%$. The residual is one-signed --- measurement always slightly above
prediction --- which is what \S\ref{sec:offaxis} leads one to expect: the cap
model is parallax-only, and perspective adds anisotropy of its own that a cap of
ray directions does not contain. The largest residual, grazing at
$\instGrazingAniso\times$ against a predicted $\instGrazingCap\times$, is the
protocol built to maximise obliquity, which is the same statement.}

So gates G3 and G4 are both passed, and the regime table of
\S\ref{sec:anisotropy} is confirmed as a measurement rather than left as a
prediction --- before a line of method code exists. This is the cheapest
falsification the project had available, and it did not fire.

\paragraph{One prediction not borne out.} Section~\ref{sec:defects} argues that
mixing cameras is the third defect of the scalar filter, and \S\ref{sec:stress}
expected the mixed-focal protocol to expose it. It does not show up in the
anisotropy: $1.16\times$ against $1.14\times$ is nothing. It does show up in the
magnitude --- the median ratio of estimation term to floor rises from $0.35$ to
$0.47$, a $35\,\%$ relative shift --- but that is still far below unity, so the
floor continues to bind and Proposition~\ref{prop:reduction} still applies. The
honest reading is that the mixed-camera defect is a statement about the
\emph{value} of $f_k$, not about its isotropy, and that this protocol as
constructed does not stress it hard enough to matter. Either the protocol needs
a wider focal spread than $2$--$4\times$, or the defect is real but small; the
present measurement does not distinguish those, and it is not reported as though
it did.

\subsection*{Getting there: three bugs the parity requirement caught}
\label{sec:b1bugs}

The first implementation of B1 reported exactly what
\S\ref{sec:instrumentresults} predicts internally --- the floor binding on
$100\,\%$ of primitives, the filter isotropic to two decimals --- and still
differed from Mip-Splatting by up to $1.22$\,dB, thirty times the seed spread of
\S\ref{sec:spread}. Section~\ref{ch:discussion} names that case in advance: a
loss on the full-orbit row is a bug, not a result, because
Proposition~\ref{prop:reduction} forbids it. Diagnosing it took three passes,
and the sequence is worth recording because each step ruled something out.

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.4em,itemsep=3pt]
  \item \textbf{The floor.} B1 re-derives $f_k$ from the camera geometry, so the
        first suspect was that its floor was not Mip-Splatting's. A cross-check
        was added --- \texttt{compute\_3D\_filter} runs alongside and the two are
        compared on every call --- and they agree to $1.16\times10^{-10}$
        absolute, $10^{-7}$ relative, for the whole run. Not the cause. B1 now
        takes their floor verbatim anyway, since \S\ref{sec:proposition2} floors
        at ``Mip-Splatting's own value'' and a re-derivation is a needless
        opportunity to differ.
  \item \textbf{The covariance path.} B1 passed $\Sigma + \Sigma_{\mathrm{filt}}$
        through the rasteriser's precomputed-covariance input while
        Mip-Splatting passes scales and rotations. The two are algebraically the
        same, but they are different code. Where the filter is isotropic on
        every primitive, B1 now takes Mip-Splatting's own path ---
        Proposition~\ref{prop:reduction} used as a computational identity rather
        than only as an argument. The gap did not move: $1.245$\,dB. Not the
        cause either.
  \item \textbf{The opacity.} What both paths share is
        Equation~\eqref{eq:filter}'s compensation, and it contained
        \texttt{clamp\_min(1e-12)} on both determinants. $|\Sigma|$ is
        $\prod s_i^2$: a primitive with $s = 0.005$ has $|\Sigma| = 1.6\times
        10^{-14}$, and one with $s=0.001$ has $10^{-18}$. Both determinants
        clamped to the same floor, their ratio became exactly $1$, and the
        compensation switched itself off for most of the model. B1 was rendering
        with uncompensated opacity while every baseline number in this thesis
        was produced with it --- which is precisely the observed signature:
        denser and sharper at full resolution, worse at every reduced scale.
\end{enumerate}

With the clamp reduced to a division guard, $|\Sigma|$ taken from the scale
vector where $\prod s_i^2$ is exact, and the isotropic regime using
Mip-Splatting's own scalar expression, the largest difference over the two scenes
then measured fell from $1.245$\,dB to $0.136$\,dB. On the full eight-scene
benchmark it is now $\mathbf{\methodParityMax}$\,dB, an order of magnitude below
the seed spread of \S\ref{sec:spread} --- the two-scene figure was itself
dominated by which two scenes they were.

\keybox{what made this findable}{%
The unit test of \S\ref{sec:proposition2} passed throughout, because its scales
were $0.01$--$0.06$, where $\prod s_i^2$ sits at the clamp and the fault is
nearly invisible. A test whose inputs do not span the real range will certify a
broken component. It now covers $|\Sigma|$ down to $1.4\times10^{-18}$ and prints
that range in its own output. The bug itself was silent --- no error, plausible
numbers, the wrong shape --- and what exposed it was requiring parity to hold
\emph{exactly} rather than approximately.}

\subsection*{What the table says}

Read down the \emph{above floor} column and the $\Delta$ column together and the
result is a single sentence: \textbf{the method does nothing on five protocols
and helps on the sixth, and the five where it does nothing are exactly the five
where Proposition~\ref{prop:reduction} says it must.}

On the full orbit, the grazing orbit, the mixed-focal capture, the one-sided arc
and the count-based cone, the estimation term exceeds the Nyquist floor on
\emph{zero} primitives. $\Sigma_{\mathrm{filt}} = f_k^2 I$ identically, the mean
anisotropy is $1.00\times$ to two decimals, and the two arms take the same code
path. The measured differences over those five rows run from $-0.041$ to
$+0.017$\,dB --- every one inside the harness's own run-to-run spread, and none
exceeding $0.8\sigma$. That
is not a weak result; it is the proposition holding, on five independently
degraded captures, to the precision of the instrument.

On the narrow pencil the filter engages for the first time in this project: the
estimation term exceeds the floor on $93\,\%$ of primitives and the filter comes
out genuinely anisotropic at $1.47\times$. And there B1 is ahead --- by
$+0.197$\,dB on the mean --- four times the run-to-run spread --- and, more to
the point, \emph{on every one of the eight paired cells}, two scenes by four test
scales, with per-cell gains from $+0.163$ to $+0.217$\,dB. A sign that is
consistent across all eight pairings is not a mean that happens to come out
positive.

\keybox{the ordering was predicted before the runs, and it is the whole claim}{%
Section~\ref{sec:anisotropy} does not predict that this method helps. It predicts
\emph{where}: nowhere that the Nyquist floor dominates, and increasingly as the
capture's angular span closes. A method that helped uniformly would contradict
its own derivation, and \S\ref{ch:discussion} recorded that in advance as a
reason for suspicion rather than celebration. What the table shows is the
predicted step: five rows inside the noise, one at four times it, and the step
falls exactly at the span where the estimation term overtakes the floor. The
conditioning column rises monotonically alongside it --- $1.17$, $1.22$, $1.48$,
$2.47$, $4.11$, $6.17\times$ --- and is measured on the same captures the PSNR
is, which is what \S\ref{sec:branchdefect} cost and bought. The
crossover is between the count-based cone at $54^\circ$ and the pencil at
$29^\circ$, and the suite contains both only because the two protocol
definitions of \S\ref{sec:branchdefect} disagreed and both were kept.}

\subsection*{What it does not say}

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.4em,itemsep=3pt]
  \item \textbf{The regime is narrow, and the absolute numbers say so.} A
        three-camera capture reconstructs badly: both arms sit near $14$\,dB on
        the pencil, against $34$ on the full orbit. The gain is real and it is
        measured on a reconstruction nobody would ship. What this establishes is
        that the mechanism is the one claimed --- the filter acts when and only
        when the estimation term binds --- not that the method is useful at this
        operating point.
  \item \textbf{Two scenes, one seed.} The eight consistent pairings are eight
        cells of one run, not eight independent experiments. The seed spread of
        \S\ref{sec:spread} bounds the noise on a cell; it does not bound the
        scene-to-scene variation of the effect, and two scenes cannot.
  \item \textbf{The count-based cone is a null result, not a missing one.} At
        $54^\circ$ the floor still binds everywhere and the method is provably
        inert. That row is evidence about where the crossover is, and it is the
        reason the crossover can be stated as a measurement rather than as an
        extrapolation from the pencil alone.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection*{An amendment to Equation~\eqref{eq:lmax}, from measurement}
\label{sec:b2amend}

As \S\ref{ch:method2} writes it, the criterion thresholds
$\lambda_{\min}(A_k(\ell))$ against an absolute $\tau$. Measured, that does not
work across regimes. $\lambda_{\min}$ carries units and scales with the angular
spread of the views, so one $\tau$ cannot mean the same thing on a 100-camera
orbit and a 10-camera cone. On \texttt{lego}:

At $\tau = 0.01$ the absolute form keeps everything on a full orbit and
\emph{nothing} on either degraded protocol; at $\tau = 0.001$ it keeps $82\,\%$
on the arc, which is nearly everything. It is a cliff, not a graded response.
The condition number $\lambda_{\min}/\lambda_{\max}$ is scale-free and behaves as
the criterion should at a single threshold, which is why it is the default in the
implementation and why Equation~\eqref{eq:lmax} should be restated in that form.
This is a change to the method that only measurement could have produced, and it
is recorded as such rather than folded silently into the derivation.

\subsection*{The comparison, on a capture where B2 prunes --- and it loses}
\label{sec:b2loses}

Restricting the cameras the \emph{criterion} reads to the low-parallax cone, and
leaving the test set whole, B2 retains $13$--$20\,\%$ of non-DC coefficients.
Magnitude pruning is then matched to that exact fraction, so the two models are
the same size and differ only in which coefficients survive.

\input{generated/table-b2-matched}

\keybox{B2, as specified, is worse than the trivial baseline}{%
On every configuration where both methods actually prune, magnitude pruning wins:
by $0.60$ and $0.82$\,dB on \texttt{lego} and by $1.82$\,dB on \texttt{chair}.
Chapter~\ref{ch:method2} states the standard this was to be held to --- ``a
criterion that cannot beat magnitude pruning at matched size is not worth the
machinery'' --- and by that standard the criterion fails. The row at $0\,\%$ is
both methods discarding everything and agreeing by definition; it is not
evidence either way.}

\paragraph{Why, and what survives.} Identifiability and importance are different
quantities, and the metric rewards the second. A coefficient can be badly
conditioned by the capture and still carry most of the fitted energy for that
primitive; zeroing it costs more PSNR than zeroing a well-determined coefficient
that happens to be tiny. Magnitude pruning optimises directly for what PSNR
measures, and B2 optimises for something else and is then scored on PSNR.

There is a second, more mundane asymmetry, and it should be ruled in rather than
out: B2 masks whole \emph{degrees}, so it removes coefficients in blocks of
$2\ell+1$, while magnitude pruning selects individual coefficients within the
same budget. Finer granularity at matched size is an advantage independent of the
criterion, and this experiment does not separate the two effects. A degree-blocked
magnitude control would, and has not been run.

What survives is narrower than what was claimed. The criterion still does what
\S\ref{sec:b2scale} says it does --- it responds gradually to the conditioning,
retaining $100\,\%$, $62\,\%$ and $25\,\%$ as the capture degrades --- and that
is a statement about the \emph{geometry}, measured and not in doubt. What is not
supported is the step from there to rendered quality at matched size. On the
evidence here, identifiability is a good description of what the capture
determines and a poor rule for deciding what to keep.

\paragraph{No memory saving is claimed.} Model size does not move either, and
cannot: masking coefficients to zero leaves a fixed-width PLY the same size on
disk. Realising the saving needs variable-length SH storage, which
Chapter~\ref{ch:plan} schedules for Spring. The $74.8$\,MB in all three rows is
the same file written three times.

\section{What these results do and do not establish}
They establish a control. Both baselines now exist as this project's own
measurements, on known hardware, from pinned commits, with the arm separation
verified on the saved models rather than argued from source. Every later
comparison is against these numbers and not against the published ones, which is
the L3 discipline of Table~\ref{tab:levels}.

About the proposed method they establish three things and leave one open. B1 is
implemented and reduces to its baseline exactly where the theory says it must,
verified to $1.4\times10^{-21}$ in the unit test and to $\methodParityMax$\,dB
--- well below the seed spread --- over $\methodParityScenes$ scenes and four
test scales in full training runs. B2 is implemented and masks nothing on
well-conditioned captures, which is the same result in the angular domain. And
the conditioning that both methods depend on varies across capture protocols
exactly as Equation~\eqref{eq:cap} predicts.

What remains open is whether that translates into rendered quality. Table~
\ref{tab:stress}'s PSNR columns are the test, they need GPU time the weekly
quota no longer has (\S\ref{sec:budget}), and the prediction recorded against
them stands unmarked and unhedged so that the comparison, when it comes, is not
retrospective.
