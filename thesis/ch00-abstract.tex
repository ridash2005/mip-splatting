3D Gaussian Splatting reconstructs a radiance field from a finite, non-uniform
set of camera samples, but places no limit on how small a primitive may become.
Mip-Splatting supplies one: a scalar variance floor per primitive,
$f_k^2 = 0.2\,(d_{\min}/f_{\max})^2$, added in quadrature to all three axes.
This work argues that the correct limit is neither scalar nor isotropic.
Treating primitive position as a parameter estimated from noisy image
measurements, the accumulated sampling Fisher information
\[
  \Lambda_k \;=\; \sum_n w_{nk}\,(J_nW_n)^{\!\top}\,\Sigma_{\mathrm{pix}}^{-1}\,(J_nW_n)
\]
gives, by the Cram\'er--Rao bound, a direction-dependent floor on how precisely
the capture localises that primitive.

We prove three things. First, Mip-Splatting's filter is exactly the
single-view, lateral-only, isotropised special case of this expression, and its
constant $\sqrt{0.2}$ is precisely the product $s\,\sigma_{\mathrm{pix}}$ --- a
confidence radius of $0.447$ pixels. Second, for two views subtending a parallax
angle $\theta$ at the primitive, the ratio of depth to lateral uncertainty is
exactly $1/\sin(\theta/2)$, so a scalar filter under-smooths along depth by
$5.8\times$ on a $20^\circ$ arc and by $19\times$ on a $6^\circ$ cone, while
approaching correctness on a full orbit. Third, flooring the anisotropic filter
at Mip-Splatting's own value makes the method reduce to that baseline exactly
when the floor dominates, so parity on well-conditioned benchmarks is a
structural guarantee rather than an empirical hope. We additionally propose a
per-primitive angular identifiability criterion that masks spherical-harmonic
coefficients the capture cannot determine --- 76\,\% of the model's parameters.
